ISOLA ISLAND EVENT STYLE GUIDE
(from Lorsieab2's own edits; applies to every added island event except Lorsieab2's handwritten ones)

THE KEY TO THIS GUIDE
Lorsieab2's handwritten island events ("Island Event Tone Reference - Lorsieab2's Handwritten Events.txt") and
Lorsieab2's 20 rewritten example events ("Example Events - Lorsieab2 (S001-S020).txt") are the key behind every
rule below. When in doubt, read them and write like them.

1. POSE THE DILEMMA PLAINLY
   End the setup with the choice the player faces, often as the villager's own thought or a direct question:
   "Should (name) try to salvage them, or is it too late to save them?"
   "How should (cook name) respond?"
   "Could (name2) have placed this flower? (name) thinks. What should I do?"
   "(name) is afraid of getting stung, but the honey..."
   The choices then answer that question directly ("Attempt to Salvage the Scrolls" / "It Is Too Late").

2. EXPLAIN WHERE STRANGE THINGS COME FROM
   Magic and odd happenings get a cause or a source inside the story (for example, one of the elder's
   grandchildren was trying out a new spell). Mysteries may stay open to interpretation, but the event gives a
   reason to wonder about.

2a. NO CONTRADICTIONS
   Every line must agree with the rest of the event. If someone "won't explain", they don't then explain; give
   the cause through someone else ("the elders won't say any more. But the children whisper that..."). If
   something "never" happens, it doesn't happen later in the same event. An added cause, detail or line must fit
   what the setup already says. Read the whole event again after every change.

3. NAME THE PEOPLE IN THE SCENE
   Use name slots for side characters instead of vague roles: "(cook name), the head cook", "another (name)",
   "both of the (name)s". Use natural words ("wait for its parents", not "the owner"). Cut padding
   ("They decide to stay." is enough).

4. GROUNDED STAKES, NOT CUTE GIMMICKS
   Keep the humor, but make it real: a leaking roof leaves "a large mushy mess", not a stain shaped like a duck.
   Warnings are concrete ("the time it swallowed a person", "its teeth could crack a boulder"). Consequences are
   felt ("nobody blames (name) out loud, but inside they are seething"). Bad things are described just enough.

5. TRIM REPEATS
   Say a beat once ("Nobody minds." not "Nobody minds. Nobody minds anything.").

6. PRECISE, NEUTRAL WORDS
   Use the exact word (seaweed or kelp, not "weed"; condensation drops; nimbus cloud; syllable; stingers;
   "hard as a rock"). Don't assume looks: "luscious" hair, not "dark" hair (not all villagers have dark hair);
   "bending (his/her) body", not only knees; "their hat" for a stranger.

7. ENDINGS WITH A SPECIFIC PAYOFF
   Close on a concrete detail: the bird gets a name ("Sweetie"); the traveller tips (his/her) hat to (name);
   "(name) is stunned that a pig just bested (him/her) at (his/her) job...!" Never mention something the story
   never introduced.

8. EFFECTS, WRITTEN PLAINLY
   - Dislikes: "now dislikes swimming, or something similar if the game has it".
   - Injuries: "loses health" (needs the healers).
   - Pregnancy: say how many babies; twins or triplets where the game allows them.
   - Clones: one or two, each with a different name.
   - A "?" name only comes from a bump on the head or something like it (a fall, a fever, memory-taking magic);
     never from a villager choosing "?" or the village calling them "?".
   - Every ending within a choice is equally likely.

8a. LIKES AND DISLIKES ARE NOT JOBS
   Never write "(name) hates (a job), so (name) gets a dislike", or "(name) loves (a job), so a like". Say what
   (name) actually comes to like or dislike, using the game's own like/dislike (the thing itself), even when that
   is what makes them do or stop doing a job in the game: "(name) can't stand the feel of soil anymore"
   (dislike: farming), not "(name) hates being a farmer now"; "(name) can't stop admiring how things are put
   together" (like: building), not "(name) loves being a builder".

9. THIRD PERSON
   Narration is always in the third person. First or second person ("I", "you", "we") only inside what someone
   says out loud (quotes) or thinks in their own words. The game's own reward line ("Your villagers gain (award)
   food.") stays as the games write it.

10. ALWAYS
   Titles and choices in Title Case. PG-13 presentation: bad things happen, described just enough, never
   sanitized. Villagers are peaceful, imperfect people. Supernatural beings stay open to interpretation.
   In VV5, Isolans (Guardians of Isola wording).

OUTCOMES = ANY CHANGE TO THE VILLAGE, A VILLAGER OR BEHAVIOUR, OR TO TECH LEVELS, FOOD, TECH POINTS ETC.
Every kind of change the vanilla island events make in the five games (details per event below):

THE VILLAGE
- Stored food gained or lost; the berry bush, crop field, fruit trees or mushrooms filled or emptied
- Tech points gained or lost; a technology level raised (e.g. Exploration, Culture, Spirituality, Medicine)
- Huts and buildings damaged or repaired; puzzles affected
- Everyone happier, celebrating, dancing, relaxing or swimming for a while (mood and behaviour)
- Weather and seasons: rain, wind, heat, cold snaps, fog, storms (with their food or health effects)

VILLAGERS
- New villagers arrive or join (adults, children, babies, barrels of babies, visitors who stay)
- Conceptions and pregnancies, twins and triplets (where the game has them)
- Deaths with a grave, and disappearances without one
- Falling sick (one villager or many), health lost or restored
- Skills gained or lost; likes and dislikes gained or lost
- Age changes (e.g. made younger or older)
- New heads, bodies or clothes; special titles or robes (e.g. the Chief)
- Names changed or lost ("?")
- New Believers: faith gained or lost, believers turning Heathen and Heathens converting

BEHAVIOUR
- A villager or the whole village drops what they are doing for a while (celebrating, swimming, relaxing,
  mourning, dancing)
- Likes and dislikes that change which jobs and actions villagers choose (see Appendix A)
