MORE EVENTS

46. The Pufferfish Helmet — A dried, spiny pufferfish shaped into a helmet turns up among an elder's keepsakes. Nobody is sure whether it was worn, displayed or made as a joke, and the elder only smiles.
47. The Shark-Tooth Edge — A carver sets a row of shark teeth along a wooden blade for cutting fiber and cleaning fish. When an apprentice asks whether it is a tool or a weapon, the carver says it depends on whose hands hold it, and why.
48. The Armor That Became a Trellis — An old woven breastplate, too brittle to wear, is tied to a garden post, and beans now climb it. Some elders find this fitting; others wish it had been kept indoors.
49. The Speaking Staff — A carved staff passed from hand to hand at councils shows whose turn it is to speak. Its keeper says it was once a fighting staff, and the village agrees it suits its new work better.
50. The Sling for the Gardens — A teenager learns to whirl a sling so that its snap scares birds away from the sprouting taro, aiming well clear of them. The gardeners argue over whether it is the noise or the aim that works.
51. The Child's Reed Spear — A small child carries a soft reed "spear" in a festival procession, copying the adults. An elder trims its tip round and lets the child lead the line for a few proud steps.
52. The Basalt Adze — A heavy stone adze head has shaped canoes for at least four generations. Its newest keeper fits a new handle and cuts one small notch into it, as every keeper before has done.
53. The Fishhook Pendant — A grandparent's finest bone fishhook is worn as a pendant by a grandchild who has never enjoyed fishing. The family agrees the hook can be honored without being used.
54. The Feather Cloak — A cloak made of thousands of tiny feathers is unrolled for a festival. Its keepers explain that each patch was added by a different maker, and nobody remembers who began it.
55. The Shield as a Cradle — A family lines an old curved shield with soft mats so a baby can nap in it on the floor of the hut. Some relatives find it sweet; others wonder what the shield's makers would have thought.
56. The Musical Bow — An old hunting bow, too worn to hunt with, is fitted with a hollow gourd and plucked for music. Its owner says it has never sounded so cheerful.
57. The Notched Handle — The handle of an inherited fishing spear bears a notch for every repair rather than every catch. Its keeper is prouder of the repairs.
58. The Tortoiseshell Ornament — A family's ceremonial ornament is made from a turtle shell that, they say, washed ashore long ago. Villagers discuss whether shell should ever be taken from living turtles and agree that future ornaments will come only from shells the sea brings in.
59. The Whalebone Paddle — A ceremonial paddle carved from the bone of a whale that stranded generations ago has never touched the water. It is carried at the head of the canoe blessing, and a child asks whether it gets lonely on its shelf.
60. The Spearhead Pendant — A broken stone spearhead is reshaped into a pendant by an apprentice who wants the stone to stay useful in a gentler way. The spearhead's family is touched and asks to wear it on feast days.
61. The Helmet Nest — A seabird builds a nest inside a ceremonial helmet left on a high shelf. The keeper decides the festival can do without the helmet this season.
62. The Training Pads — Padded practice gear stuffed with coconut fiber is restuffed after many years of use. Tucked inside, the restorers find small charms and shells left by earlier trainees, and they put every one back.
63. The Arrow Over the Door — A family keeps an arrow with its point removed above their doorway. They say it means the household no longer needs to hunt; neighbors have three other explanations, and the family enjoys all of them.
64. The Watch Drum — An old signal drum from a lookout is now played at dances. A few elders still go quiet at its first deep beat, so the drummers learn to begin softly, and nobody is asked to explain.
65. The Throwing Stick — A curved throwing stick once used for hunting birds is now used only to knock ripe fruit loose. The children hold contests for the gentlest knock that still brings the fruit down.
66. The Bowstring Maker — The island's finest bowstrings are twisted by a villager who uses a wheelchair and works at a low, wide workbench. They have never wanted to shoot a bow; they simply love getting the twist exactly right, and apprentices line up to learn it.
67. The Helmet That Was a Bowl — Scholars argue whether an old dome of hardened wood was a helmet or a mixing bowl. A cook settles nothing by making an excellent stew in it.
68. The Retired Watch Spear — A long spear from an abandoned lookout is given to a fisher, who uses it to pole a canoe through the shallows. The fisher says it is the most useful thing the lookout ever gave the village.
69. The Restorer's Restraint — An artisan restoring an old shield leaves one deep, old gash unmended because it is part of the shield's history. The family is divided about it, and for now the gash stays.
70. The Lending Bow — The village keeps one practice bow that anyone may borrow. Its borrowing rules are carved on a board beside it, and the last rule simply says, "Bring it back in a good mood."
71. The Small Grip — An apprentice notices that an old ceremonial spear is wrapped for a very small hand. It may have been made for a child's ceremony or for a small adult; nobody knows, and the family keeps both possibilities in its telling.
72. The Staff Given to a Neighbor — A neighbor's family lost their inherited staff when a storm took their hut. Another family offers them one of its own heirloom staffs, and the two families argue kindly about whether it is a gift or a loan.
73. The Mask and the Shield — A dancer's mask and an old shield from a different household turn out to carry the same painted pattern. The two families wonder whether one hand made both, long ago, and begin trading stories.
74. The Carved Record — A tall post carries the mark of every shieldmaker who has worked in the village. A new apprentice hesitates for a whole season before adding a mark of their own.
75. The Sharpening Stone — A great grinding stone at the edge of the village is worn with grooves from generations of sharpening adzes, knives and fishhooks. Children like to trace the grooves and guess which tool made each one.
76. The Armor in the Story — An elder describes a suit of shell armor that nobody living has ever seen. Several craftspeople try to make it from the description alone, and every version comes out differently.
77. The Mended Quiver — A child mends a torn quiver with crooked, uneven stitches. Its owner, an expert archer, decides to keep the crooked stitches forever.
78. The Harvest Blade — A ceremonial blade is used only to cut the first stalk of each year's harvest. When it chips, the village debates whether a chipped blade still counts, and the oldest farmer says the stalks have never complained.
79. The Bow Nobody Can Draw — An ancestor's bow is so stiff that no living villager can draw it. Some say the ancestor was remarkably strong; others say the wood has simply hardened with age; the bow keeps its secret.
80. A New Mark on the Family Shield — A villager who joined the family as a grown adult is invited to paint their own symbol on the family shield. They take a long time choosing it, and the family waits happily.
81. The Implement Nobody Recognizes — A strange tool with three prongs and a hooked end is found in the storehouse. Guesses include fishing, weaving, gardening and defense, and the keepers finally label it "purpose unknown."
82. The Inventory Song — The keeper of the storehouse does not keep a written list of the implements; they sing a long song naming every one. An apprentice starts learning the song and finds three verses for objects that are no longer there.
83. The Spear That Became a Cane — An elder's walking cane was carved from an old spear shaft. The elder says it has finally found its true purpose.
84. The Hunter Who Put Down the Bow — An aging hunter decides they no longer wish to hunt and spends their days watching birds instead. Others still hunt for the village, and nobody treats either choice as the wrong one.
85. The Practice Shield Sled — A parent carves a small practice shield for a child, who would much rather use it to slide down the dunes. The parent laughs and carves runners for it.
86. The Many-Headed Helmet — The ceremonial helmet is padded differently for each wearer. Inside its lining are layers of padding from many festivals, each one shaped by a different head.
87. The Arrows Laid Down — At a remembrance gathering, archers lay down arrows with their points removed. Some say the custom marked the end of a troubled time long ago; others say it was always simply a sign of respect; both explanations are kept.
88. The Apprentice Who Fears Blades — A toolmaker's apprentice is nervous around sharp edges. The master starts them with handles, bindings and polishing, and the apprentice discovers they are the best binder in the workshop.
89. The Season of Swapped Heirlooms — Two families exchange heirloom spears for one season so that each can learn the other's stories. Both are glad to have them back, and both know more than they did.
90. The Measuring Spear — An old spear shaft hangs in the meeting place, notched to measure canoe lengths. Over the years, families have also marked their children's heights on it, and nobody wants to erase a single line.
91. The Shield at the Tide Line — A shield of an unfamiliar design washes up on the beach. Its maker may have lived anywhere, and the village keeps it dry and undecorated while the guesses multiply.
92. The Bowyer's Grove — A grove planted generations ago for bow wood is now old and full of birds. The village decides to take only fallen limbs from it, and the bowyers adjust their craft to suit.
93. The Armor Against the Weather — During the storm season, villagers wear thick woven mats over their heads and shoulders to fetch supplies through wind and flying branches. A child calls it "storm armor," and the name sticks.
94. The Old Drill Becomes a Game — An old training exercise has become a children's game played with padded sticks, in which tapping a shoulder counts as a point and nobody is ever struck. Elders recognize the steps and teach the children the old names for them.
95. The Heirloom Nobody Owns — A ceremonial spear belongs to the whole village, and each year a different household keeps it. This year it goes to an elder who lives alone, who sets it proudly beside the door and invites everyone in to see it.
