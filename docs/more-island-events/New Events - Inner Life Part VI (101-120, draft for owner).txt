101. Where Do the Waves Come From?
(picks any villager aged 8 or older)

(name1) is sitting on the beach watching the waves come in, one after another, and a question has got stuck in (his/her) head. Where does each wave start? Somewhere out there, past where (he/she) can see, every one of these waves must have begun. Is there a place where the sea is flat and calm and then, all of a sudden, decides to make a wave?

Ask (name2):
(name2) thinks it's the wind pushing the water. (name1) points out that there's hardly any wind today, and the waves are still coming. (name2) thinks some more. "Wind from somewhere else, then." "Where?" "...Somewhere windy." They agree to watch the horizon for a windy-looking place. They don't find one.

Follow One Wave All the Way In:
(name1) picks a wave far out and watches it all the way to the shore. It rises, curls, and breaks right at (his/her) feet, soaking (his/her) knees. (name1) decides that wherever it came from, it had been aiming for (him/her) the whole time.

102. Do Fish Sleep?
(picks any villager aged 8 or older)

(name1) has been lying awake wondering whether fish sleep. They don't have eyelids. They're always moving. But they must get tired, surely? After a whole day of swimming? Before dawn, (name1) gets up, takes a torch and goes down to the tide pools to find out.

Watch Quietly:
(name1) crouches by a tide pool, very still. The little fish hang in the water, barely moving, tucked under ledges. Are they asleep? Are they just thinking? One of them drifts sideways and gives a small, startled twitch, exactly the way (name1) does when (he/she) dozes off by the fire. (name1) decides that's a yes.

Wake (name2) to Come and See:
(name2) is not delighted to be woken. (He/she) comes anyway, wrapped in a blanket and grumbling. They crouch over the pool together. "Are they asleep?" whispers (name1). "I was," whispers (name2). Then (he/she) sees a fish slowly bump into a rock and drift away without noticing. They both have to cover their mouths so they don't laugh and wake it.

103. Why Does the Fire Crackle?
(picks any villager aged 8 or older)

(name1) is sitting by the fire, and the fire is doing what it always does: crackling, popping, now and then spitting a little spark. Tonight (name1) wonders why. Is it the wood? Is it something inside the wood? Does dry wood crackle less than wet wood? Does every kind of wood sound different?

Test Different Woods:
(name1) collects a little pile of different woods (driftwood, palm, a dry stick, a damp one) and adds them one at a time, listening carefully. The damp one hisses and pops like an angry crab. The driftwood barely makes a sound. (name1) is so absorbed that (he/she) doesn't notice the others have stopped talking to listen too. By the end, the whole fire is guessing which piece will be loudest. [effect: small tech gain, only where the game has tech points]

Just Listen:
(name1) doesn't test anything. (He/she) just listens: the little snaps, the long whispers, the sudden pop that makes everyone jump. It sounds a bit like the fire is talking to itself. (name1) decides that's as good an explanation as any, and doesn't interrupt.

"It's shrinking," (name1) whispers. "Where's it going?" (name2) looks up at the sky. (name1) looks up too. Neither of them can quite believe it.

(picks any villager aged 5 or older)

(name1) has found a big curly shell up near the gardens, far from the beach. (He/she) holds it to (his/her) ear, the way children do, and hears the sea: a soft, rushing, faraway roar. (name1) decides not to work it out. (He/she) takes the shell home and keeps it by (his/her) sleeping mat. On nights when (he/she) can't sleep, (he/she) holds it to (his/her) ear and listens to the sea that isn't there, and falls asleep before (he/she) can wonder about it too much.

106. The Stars Go Round
(picks any villager aged 12 or older)

Lying on the beach one night, (name1) notices a bright star just above a palm tree. Much later, (he/she) looks again. The star has moved. In fact, all of them have moved, slowly, together, as though the whole sky is turning around one point.

Stay Up and Watch:
(name1) stays awake almost all night, marking the stars against the trees. By dawn (he/she) is certain: they go round, all of them, except one small star that barely moves at all. Why that one? (He/she) falls asleep on the sand before (he/she) can decide, and wakes up with a crab on (his/her) foot.

Tell (name2) in the Morning:
(name2) listens, then asks the obvious question: "Is the sky going round, or are we?" (name1) opens (his/her) mouth and closes it again. Neither of them can say. It's a strange, dizzy thought, and they both hold on to the nearest tree trunk for a moment, just in case.

107. Blue Sea, Clear Water
(picks any villager aged 8 or older)

The sea is blue. Everyone knows that. But when (name1) scoops it up in (his/her) hands, the water is perfectly clear. (He/she) tries again. Clear. (He/she) carries a full bowl up the beach and looks at it. Clear. The sea, just behind (him/her), is as blue as ever.

Ask (name2):
"Maybe it's the sky," says (name2). "Maybe the sea is just reflecting it." "But on cloudy days it's still sort of blue-grey." "Maybe it remembers being blue." They look at the bowl of water. It doesn't seem to remember anything. They pour it back, to be on the safe side.

Try a Bigger Bowl:
(name1) decides the problem is the size of the bowl. A big enough bowl must turn blue. (He/she) fills the biggest pot in the village. It is, very faintly, a tiny bit blue. Maybe. If you squint. (name1) counts this as proof of something, (he/she) just isn't sure what.

108. Which Way Is Up?
(picks any villager aged 8 or older)

(name1) has always wondered how a seed knows which way to grow. It's in the dark, underground. Nobody tells it where the sky is. So, as an experiment, (he/she) plants three seeds the right way up and three seeds upside down, and marks each one with a little stick.

(name1) carefully uncovers all six. Every one of them, even the upside-down ones, has sent its shoot curling round to point straight up, and its root straight down. (name1) sits back on (his/her) heels, amazed. The seeds know. (He/she) has no idea how. That's somehow even better. [effect: small Farming or Science skill, only where the game has a fitting one]

Wait and See What Comes Up:
(name1) waits. All six seeds come up, perfectly normal, pointing at the sky. (He/she) can't tell which ones were upside down anymore. (name2) suggests that maybe the seeds just turned round when nobody was looking. (name1) thinks that's exactly what happened, and wishes (he/she) had been looking.

(name1) has been arguing with (him/her)self all day about what the biggest living thing on Isola is. A whale? (He/she) has seen one from the shore, and it was enormous. (picks a child aged 4-9)

"Where does the wind live?" (name1) asks. (name2) opens (his/her) mouth to answer, and realizes (he/she) has no idea. "It must go home sometimes," (name1) points out. "It's not windy at night always."

112. How Many Leaves?
(picks any villager aged 10 or older)

(name1) is lying under a tree and wonders how many leaves are on it. Hundreds? Thousands? There must be an exact number. Right now, this very moment, there is one true number of leaves on this tree, and nobody knows it. This bothers (name1) more than it probably should.

Start Counting:
(name1) starts with the lowest branch. One, two, three... At four hundred and twelve, a breeze makes the branch wave and (he/she) loses (his/her) place. (He/she) starts again. At about two hundred, a leaf falls off. Does that count? (name1) gives up and decides the tree has "lots."

Estimate:
(name1) counts the leaves on one small branch. Then counts the small branches on one big branch. Then counts the big branches. Then multiplies, carefully, with marks in the sand. The answer is enormous. (He/she) proudly tells (name2). (name2) points out it's a different tree. (name1) decides that one has "about the same."

115. Counting the Thunder
(picks any villager aged 10 or older)

A storm is rolling around out at sea, a long way off. (name1) has noticed something: after the lightning flashes, there's a gap before the thunder comes. Sometimes a long gap. Sometimes a short one. (He/she) starts counting, under (his/her) breath, between the flash and the rumble.

Eight. Seven. Seven. Five. The gaps are getting shorter. "I think the storm's coming closer," (name1) says. (name2) looks at the sky, then at (name1). "How do you know?" "The counting told me." They move the drying fish inside, just in case. A little later, the rain arrives right on time.

Ask (name2) If (he/she) Has Noticed It:
(name2) has noticed, (he/she) just never thought about it. "Maybe the thunder is slower than the light," (he/she) suggests. "Maybe it walks and the light runs." (name1) likes that very much. They sit under the eaves together, counting and guessing, until the storm drifts off the other way.

(picks two adults)

At dinner, (name1) yawns. Across the fire, (name2) yawns. Then someone else does, and then a child, and then, (name1) is almost sure, a dog lying by the fire. (name1) sits up. "Did you see that? It went all the way round."

Soon the entire fire is yawning, and nobody can stop, and (name1) is yawning so much (he/she) can't explain the experiment. Everybody goes to bed very early. 117. The Birds That Leave
(picks any villager aged 8 or older)

Every year, around the same time, a certain kind of long-winged bird gathers in great noisy flocks on the cliffs, and then, one morning, they're all gone. Months later they come back. (name1) has watched it happen for years without thinking about it. This year, watching the last of them fly off over the horizon, (he/she) suddenly wonders: where do they go?

Ask (name2) What (he/she) Thinks:
(name2) thinks they go somewhere warmer. Or colder. Or somewhere with better fish. "Somewhere beyond the sea," (he/she) says. "Somewhere we've never seen." They watch the empty sky for a while. (name1) finds it strange and wonderful to think that the same birds that sit on Isola's cliffs have seen places nobody here ever will.

Wait for Them to Come Back:
(name1) marks the day they left with a notch on (his/her) doorpost. When the birds come back, months later, (he/she) marks that day too. (He/she) has no idea where they went or what they saw. But when the first one lands on the cliff, tired and noisy and home again, (name1) waves at it as though it's an old friend.

118. The Tide
(picks an adult and an elder)

(name1) has started keeping track of the tide. It comes in and goes out, twice a day, but never at quite the same time. Every day it's a little later. (He/she) asks (name2), an elder, why. (name2) says the moon pulls the sea, the way you'd pull a blanket. (name1) has heard another villager say the sea is breathing.

Ask How (name2) Knows:
"My teacher told me," says (name2). "And the tide does seem to follow the moon. Watch, and see if I'm right." (name1) watches for a month. The tide and the moon do seem to dance together. (name1) still can't picture how the moon could pull anything from so far away. It goes on being wonderful and confusing at the same time.

Keep Both Ideas:
(name1) decides (he/she) likes both: the moon pulling the sea like a blanket, and the sea slowly breathing in and out. Maybe one is true. Maybe neither. Maybe they're two ways of saying the same thing. Either way, (he/she) stops being surprised when the tide is late. It's just taking its time.
