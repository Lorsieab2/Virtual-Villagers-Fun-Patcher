ISOLA — BEYOND THE HORIZON: THE WORLD WE IMAGINE
A NEW, SEPARATE COMPANION EVENT ANTHOLOGY FOR VIRTUAL VILLAGERS 1–5
Version 1.0 | 40 original branching events

CREATIVE PREMISE
The villagers know their own lives; they do not know what exists beyond the horizon. Every account of distant societies, technology, nature, or institutions in this anthology is a hypothesis, story, analogy, dream, speculation, or disputed interpretation—not confirmation of a modern outside world. Even an odd artifact cannot prove a particular civilization exists. Crucially, villagers ask not just “How different are they?” but “Might they live as we do?” They imagine distant people eating, fishing, farming, raising children, studying, working, playing, loving, quarreling, grieving, celebrating, and growing old.

EDITORIAL AND IMPLEMENTATION RULES
- Separate companion anthology. Do not overwrite or alter the Grand Event Collection or earlier companion collections.
- Applicable to VV1–VV5 unless a specific variant is stated. VV1 Southern Shore; VV2 Western Shore; VV3 Secret City (Nature/Magic factions only here); VV4 Tree of Life; VV5 New Believers (respect the distinction between the player's tribe and the Heathens/Isolans).
- Villagers are capable thinkers, not comic caricatures of people who lack modern technology. They may invent sophisticated theories but cannot know unknown facts by magic of narration.
- Every choice has two mutually exclusive possible outcomes. Choose one outcome from the selected choice, never both. Story-only NPCs and imagined distant peoples are not spawned or confirmed.
- Rewards, where given, should be small, game-scaled Research/Science, Devotion, Healing, Farming, Building, Parenting, or food/tech gains as supported; quiet scenes may give none. Never invent mechanics, sprites, or permanent structures.
- No mandatory happy endings. PG-13 and non-graphic. Treat disability, skin and hair diversity, bodies, cultures, belief, and differences respectfully. Normal human variation alone does not require a content warning.
- All statements about distant societies remain conditional: might, perhaps, some imagine, if such places exist. A villager may be wrong; the event does not settle the truth.

FORMAT: Setup; A/B/C choices; two alternate outcomes per choice; optional note.

PART I — ARE THEY LIKE US? EVERYDAY LIFE BEYOND THE SEA
====================================================================

EVENT 001 — THE OTHER BREAKFAST
SETUP: While sharing roasted fruit, a child asks whether families beyond the ocean wake up hungry and argue about breakfast too.
A. Imagine familiar meals.
 A1. The children invent a distant family who burns their morning bread and laughs; the cook admits that sounds entirely plausible.
 A2. An elder remembers that even neighboring households eat differently; the group sketches many possible breakfasts instead of one.
B. Imagine unfamiliar foods.
 B1. A gardener describes grains, roots, and creatures they have never seen; everyone grows hungry during the discussion.
 B2. The wild guesses turn into an argument over what can safely be eaten; a healer reminds them that novelty is not proof of nourishment.
C. Ask what meals mean.
 C1. A parent suggests that sharing food might comfort people anywhere, whatever is on the plate.
 C2. A solitary villager says some people may eat alone by preference or necessity; the table falls thoughtfully quiet.

EVENT 002 — DO THEIR CHILDREN ASK WHY?
SETUP: A teacher wonders whether distant children interrupt lessons, invent games, and ask questions adults cannot answer.
A. Imagine a classroom like ours.
 A1. The children gleefully reenact a distant lesson, complete with one pupil who refuses to sit still.
 A2. The teacher realizes that some children might learn by watching family members rather than attending formal lessons.
B. Imagine an immense school.
 B1. Researchers speculate about halls full of pupils and teachers with different specialties; a young apprentice is delighted.
 B2. A shy child wonders whether a vast school would feel lonely; the teacher takes the concern seriously.
C. Ask the children themselves.
 C1. They agree that curiosity, boredom, friendship, and mischief might cross every sea.
 C2. Their imagined children are so unlike one another that the teacher abandons the idea of a single universal childhood.

EVENT 003 — SOMEONE TO COME HOME TO
SETUP: Two villagers wonder whether distant people hurry home to spouses, friends, relatives, pets, or simply a peaceful room.
A. Picture a bustling household.
 A1. A grandmother imagines several generations arguing cheerfully over the same cooking fire.
 A2. A villager raised in a small household points out that crowded homes can be comforting or exhausting.
B. Picture someone living alone.
 B1. The group imagines a content craftsperson enjoying privacy after work.
 B2. Someone asks whether a solitary person might want company but have trouble finding it; nobody dismisses the possibility.
C. Discuss what makes a home.
 C1. They decide that care and safety may matter more than a dwelling's size.
 C2. A builder says shelter and reliable water matter too; the conversation turns practical.

EVENT 004 — THE ARGUMENT ACROSS THE OCEAN
SETUP: After two neighbors dispute a borrowed basket, a mediator asks whether people in faraway lands quarrel over equally ordinary things.
A. Imagine the same disagreement.
 A1. The villagers act out a distant argument over a missing tool; everyone recognizes the irritation.
 A2. The mediator notes that different customs may change what counts as borrowing or stealing.
B. Imagine enormous disputes.
 B1. A scholar wonders whether disagreements between whole cities could begin with ordinary misunderstandings.
 B2. A fisher warns that conflicts over land and food may have causes too serious to reduce to hurt feelings.
C. Imagine reconciliation.
 C1. Two estranged neighbors exchange a tentative greeting after hearing their own behavior described.
 C2. The original dispute remains unresolved; the mediator says understanding does not compel forgiveness.

EVENT 005 — DO THEY MOURN AS WE DO?
SETUP: Preparing a remembrance meal, a young villager wonders whether distant peoples remember their dead with songs, silence, flowers, stories, or nothing at all.
A. Imagine familiar ceremonies.
 A1. An elder suggests that people elsewhere may gather around names and memories just as the village does.
 A2. A parent says some mourn privately and would not welcome a gathering; the preparations become more considerate.
B. Imagine unfamiliar customs.
 B1. A storyteller invents a distant tradition of floating lights; the listeners find it moving but acknowledge it is imaginary.
 B2. The story unsettles a mourner who prefers familiar rites; the group allows the subject to rest.
C. Ask what grief shares.
 C1. A healer proposes that longing for someone absent might be recognizable across cultures.
 C2. Another villager wonders whether people experience loss in radically different ways; the question stays open.
Content note: grief and bereavement.

EVENT 006 — A DAY WITHOUT ANYTHING REMARKABLE
SETUP: A child assumes life beyond Isola must be an endless adventure. A tired farmer doubts it.
A. Imagine an ordinary working day.
 A1. They invent a distant worker who misplaces a tool, eats lunch, and comes home tired; the child is disappointed and amused.
 A2. The farmer wonders whether some people work at night or indoors; the group considers how many routines are possible.
B. Imagine a day of leisure.
 B1. The children invent festivals, games, books, and quiet afternoons for people they will never meet.
 B2. A caregiver asks who does the necessary work while others rest; the conversation becomes a debate about fairness.
C. Ask whether ordinary days matter.
 C1. A villager says that a peaceful uneventful day can be a gift after hardship.
 C2. Another admits that sameness can feel stifling; both perspectives are allowed to stand.

EVENT 007 — THE PEOPLE WHO GROW OLD
SETUP: Watching an elder mend a net, an apprentice asks whether people in distant lands also lose strength, gain experience, and wonder where the years went.
A. Imagine elders surrounded by family.
 A1. The apprentice imagines grandchildren learning an old song; the elder teaches the real song at once.
 A2. The elder notes that some people have no children and still deserve friendship and care.
B. Imagine distant methods of care.
 B1. Healers speculate about specialized houses and tools that might help people move or hear.
 B2. Someone worries that specialized care could separate elders from familiar places; no one declares one arrangement best.
C. Ask the elder directly.
 C1. The elder says being listened to matters more than being praised for wisdom.
 C2. The elder laughs and says not every old person is wise; the apprentice makes a note of that too.

EVENT 008 — THE OTHER SIDE OF A CELEBRATION
SETUP: During preparations for a village feast, a musician wonders what distant people celebrate and whether their festivals also go wrong.
A. Imagine shared milestones.
 A1. Births, partnerships, harvests, and reunions become the subjects of an improvised song.
 A2. Someone points out that not everyone celebrates the same milestones or wants a public ceremony.
B. Imagine unfamiliar festivals.
 B1. The children design a spectacular hypothetical procession of lights and painted cloth.
 B2. A practical organizer calculates the food and cleanup required and declares the imaginary festival already over budget.
C. Imagine a festival disaster.
 C1. The musicians perform a deliberately mistimed entrance; the crowd laughs.
 C2. A villager recalls a real celebration spoiled by conflict, and the group decides to plan a quieter gathering.

 B2. An elder wonders how someone unable to climb steps would reach the top; accessibility becomes central to the design.
C. Compare with island homes.
 C1. The builders admire the potential to shelter many people on limited land.
 C2. A farmer worries about gardens, waste, and fresh air; the imagined city grows more complicated.

C. Imagine complete silence.
 C1. The group concludes that even a huge city might contain quiet rooms and sleeping children.
 C2. A villager who struggles with noise wonders whether quiet could be difficult to find in crowded places.

C. Ask what happens to leftovers.

EVENT 012 — A JOB NO ONE CAN SEE
SETUP: A fisher wonders whether distant people might spend their days doing work that produces no visible fish, baskets, houses, or crops.
A. Imagine knowledge work.
 A1. Researchers invent professions devoted to counting, organizing, teaching, planning, and solving disputes.

B. Imagine caring professions.
 B1. A parent proposes that listening, teaching, nursing, and supporting others are real work even when nothing tangible remains.
 B2. Someone asks why care is sometimes overlooked when it is unpaid; a lively discussion follows.
C. Imagine work chosen freely.
 C1. An apprentice dreams of learning several crafts rather than inheriting one.
 C2. A villager warns that choice may be limited by family obligations, illness, or scarcity.

EVENT 013 — ROADS WITHOUT FOOTPRINTS
SETUP: A navigator wonders whether distant peoples travel over land in vessels pulled by animals—or moved by some unknown force.
A. Imagine swift vehicles.
 A1. Children design wheeled carts that race faster than a running villager, at least in their drawings.
 A2. The builders discover that speed creates problems of steering, stopping, and safe crossings.
B. Imagine public transport.
 B1. The council imagines a shared vehicle carrying strangers to common destinations.
 B2. A parent asks how a tired child or disabled traveler would board; the plan is revised.
C. Imagine walking instead.
 C1. A farmer says even distant cities may contain people who prefer to walk.
 C2. A traveler notes that long distances might make walking impossible for some; they debate equitable access.

EVENT 014 — THE SKY FULL OF TRAVELERS
SETUP: A child watches seabirds and asks whether people elsewhere might build machines to cross the sky.
A. Consider wings.
 A1. The researchers study lift in bird wings and make a small harmless gliding model.
 A2. Their first model dives straight into the sand; the children demand another trial.
B. Consider floating craft.
 B1. A scholar proposes a great bag of heated air carrying a basket, then admits many practical questions remain.
 B2. The village debates whether storms would make such travel too dangerous.
C. Ask why people would fly.
 C1. A parent imagines distant relatives reaching one another sooner.
 C2. A fisher wonders whether rapid travel could also spread conflict or disease; the question sobers the group.

EVENT 015 — WHO CLEANS THE GREAT CITY?
SETUP: After clearing refuse from the shore, a sanitation worker asks how an imagined city of thousands could keep its water and streets clean.
A. Design water channels.
 A1. Builders sketch separate routes for drinking water and waste; the discussion improves their own sanitation practices.
 A2. The proposed channel threatens a garden; the group learns that infrastructure has consequences.
B. Imagine an organized cleaning service.
 B1. Children appoint themselves inspectors and find an embarrassing number of neglected baskets.
 B2. A worker asks whether the cleaners receive fair pay and protection; the council discusses unseen labor.
C. Imagine neglect.
 C1. A healer explains how contamination might make crowded communities vulnerable to illness.
 C2. The village remembers its own lapses and quietly organizes a cleanup rather than mocking imaginary outsiders.

EVENT 016 — THE HOUSE WITH NO GARDEN
SETUP: A gardener wonders whether people in distant cities might live far from the soil that feeds them.
A. Imagine distant farms.
 A1. They theorize that food might travel between communities through elaborate trade networks.
 A2. A farmer worries that storms or broken routes could leave city dwellers hungry.
B. Imagine gardens in strange places.
 B1. The children design gardens on roofs and balconies, delighting the gardeners.
 B2. The builder questions whether the roofs can hold wet soil; the design returns to the drawing board.
C. Imagine no interest in farming.
 C1. A scholar says some people might devote themselves to other skills and still respect farmers.
 C2. A farmer fears that people separated from food production may forget how difficult it is.

C. Ask whether tools replace judgment.
 C1. The group concludes that careful listening and observation would still matter.

 B1. Researchers wonder whether controlled exposure or other techniques could train the body to resist disease, but acknowledge the idea is untested here.
 B2. The healer refuses to experiment on villagers without evidence and consent; the council approves the caution.

CREATIVE AND CANON GUIDANCE
These additions preserve the original forty events verbatim. They are a
separate expansion within the same companion anthology, not a replacement
for any existing Grand Event Collection or patcher content.

ALL OUTSIDE-WORLD SCENARIOS ARE SPECULATION. No distant machine, robot,
modern city, institution, satellite, factory, or society is established
as fact. Even descriptions of an artificial being's words, feelings, or
behavior are imagined scenarios, not confirmed encounters. The villagers
may propose scientific, spiritual, naturalistic, philosophical, or hybrid
interpretations without the narrator declaring one correct. Do not equate
sophisticated speech with proven consciousness or dismiss sentience as
impossible. A robot may be understood as a construct, servant, tool,
companion, automaton, instrument, or potential moral subject; the tension
between these categories is the point.

Keep an ancient tropical, Polynesian-inspired tribal frame: councils,
elders, craftspeople, healers, fishers, gardeners, navigators, teachers,
children, researchers, spiritual thinkers, and storytellers reason from
their own sophisticated experience. They can imagine extraordinary things
without magically knowing modern names. Parenthetical modern labels in
editorial notes are for the player and event designer, not dialogue.

Each A/B/C player choice leads to exactly ONE of two mutually exclusive
outcomes. Use only story-supported villagers and game mechanics; no new
robot sprites, NPCs, structures, equipment, or AI systems are required.
Events may grant modest game-supported skill/Tech/food rewards or none.
VV1 Southern Shore; VV2 Western Shore; VV3 Secret City (Nature/Magic only
where relevant); VV4 Tree of Life; VV5 New Believers (both communities
retain autonomy). All events apply VV1–VV5 unless stated otherwise.
PG-13; varied humor, tenderness, tension, uncertainty, and disagreement.
No automatic happy endings or moral conclusions. Villagers should ask
whether outsiders may live SIMILARLY to them as often as they imagine
spectacular differences. No prejudice endorsed; no difference in body,
disability, culture, or belief treated as inherently warning-worthy.
