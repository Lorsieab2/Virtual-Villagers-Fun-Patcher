61. Old Wobbly and Friends
(picks an adult)

(name1) has given every one of (his/her) tools a name. The digging stick is Grumble. The big basket is Old Wobbly. The little knife is Sir Sharp, and the water jar, for reasons nobody remembers, is Auntie. Today (name2) asks to borrow "a basket," and (name1) looks genuinely confused. "Which one? Old Wobbly or the Twins?"

Introduce (name2) to Everyone:
(name1) brings out each tool and explains its name, its history and its mood. Grumble is reliable but sulky. The Twins must never be separated. (name2) listens to all of it with a perfectly serious face, then carefully carries Old Wobbly away with both hands. It comes back that evening full of fruit and, (name1) is fairly sure, in a better mood than before.

Just Hand Over a Basket:
(name1) hands over Old Wobbly without a word. (name2) returns it at dinner. "Thanks for the basket," (he/she) says. "Old Wobbly," (name1) corrects, before (he/she) can stop (him/her)self. There is a short pause. "Thanks for Old Wobbly," says (name2), and that is the end of it.

62. The Same Breakfast
(picks an adult)

For as long as anyone can remember, (name1) has eaten exactly the same breakfast: half a coconut, three slices of fruit, and a handful of nuts, eaten in that order. This morning (name2) offers (him/her) something new, a warm, sticky little cake (he/she) made at dawn. It smells wonderful. It is not half a coconut.

Try the Cake:
(name1) takes a careful bite. Then another. Then the whole thing is gone. "That," (he/she) says, "was very good." (name2) beams. (name1) then eats half a coconut, three slices of fruit and a handful of nuts, in that order, because the cake was lovely but breakfast is breakfast.

Politely Say No:
"It smells wonderful," (name1) says, "but I'll stick with mine, thank you." (name2) shrugs and eats the cake (him/her)self, making a lot of happy noises about it. (name1) does not regret (his/her) decision. Mostly. By lunchtime (he/she) is still thinking about the cake.

63. The Hummer
(picks an adult who is working)

(name1) hums while (he/she) works. (He/she) has no idea (he/she) does it. It's always the same tune, a cheerful little thing with a bit in the middle that goes very high. Today (name2) has been working next to (him/her) all morning, and the high bit is starting to get to (him/her).

"You know you've been humming all morning?" says (name2). (name1) is astonished. "I don't hum." (He/she) goes back to work. Within a minute (he/she) is humming again. (name2) gives up and starts humming along, and by lunchtime the two of them have worked out a harmony for the high bit.

Say Nothing and Move a Little Further Away:
(name2) shifts to the other end of the field. From here the humming is soft and pleasant, like a bird a long way off. (name2) finds (him/her)self working to the rhythm of it. That night (he/she) catches (him/her)self humming the tune at dinner and stops, quite horrified.

64. Goodnight, Trees
(picks an adult and a child aged 4-10)

Every evening, on the way home, (name1) says goodnight to the trees. Not all of them. Just the ones along (his/her) path. "Goodnight, tall one. Goodnight, crooked one. Goodnight, you with the bird's nest." Tonight (name2) has been following a few steps behind, watching very closely.

Let (name2) Join In:
"Can I do one?" (name2) asks. "Of course," says (name1). "Pick one that looks tired." (name2) picks a short, scruffy palm and wishes it goodnight with tremendous seriousness. Then another, and another. They get home very late, because (name2) has decided every single tree looks tired tonight.

Explain That It's Just a Silly Habit:
"It's just something I do," (name1) says, a little embarrassed. (name2) thinks about this for a few steps. "I don't think it's silly," (he/she) says. "I think the trees like it." (name1) has no answer for that. (He/she) says goodnight to the crooked one a little louder than usual.

65. One Stone a Day
(picks an adult)

Years ago (name1) put a stone on a flat spot at the edge of the beach, for no reason at all. The next day (he/she) put another one on top. (He/she) has added one stone almost every day since. The pile is now taller than most children, and today (name2) stops beside it and asks the question (name1) has been dreading: "Why?"

Try to Explain:
(name1) opens (his/her) mouth. Closes it. Tries again. "It was one stone," (he/she) says at last, "and then it would have been rude to stop." (name2) considers the pile for a long moment, then picks up a small stone and holds it out. "May I?" (name1) thinks about it. "Just this once."

Shrug:
"No reason," says (name1), and puts today's stone on top. (name2) waits for more. There is no more. (name2) walks on, shaking (his/her) head, and (name1) stands back to admire the pile. It is, (he/she) thinks, a very good pile.

66. The Knotted String
(picks an adult or elder)

(name1) keeps a long piece of string in (his/her) hut. Whenever (he/she) has a good day, a really good one, (he/she) ties a knot in it. It's been going for many years. The string is now so long it has to be wound up like a ball of fishing line. (name2) finds it while helping to tidy and asks what it is.

Count the Knots Together:
They sit down and start counting. Some knots (name1) remembers perfectly: the day of the big catch, the day the roof didn't leak. Some (he/she) can't remember at all, and that's somehow nice too. They lose count somewhere past two hundred and start laughing. (name1) ties one more knot before (name2) leaves.

Cut a Length for (name2):
(name1) cuts a short piece of string from the end and hands it over. "For your good days," (he/she) says. (name2) looks at the empty string, puzzled. That evening the fishing goes well, dinner is excellent, and (name2) quietly ties a knot in it before bed.

67. The Weather Guess
(picks two adults)

Every morning, before (he/she) has even sat up, (name1) guesses the weather out loud. "Sunny, with a little wind after lunch." Then (he/she) scratches a mark on the wall: a line if (he/she) was right, a dot if (he/she) was wrong. After years of this, the wall is about half lines and half dots. Today (name2) hears the guess and says, "No. Rain by midday."

Make It a Contest:
They shake on it. All morning both of them keep glancing at the sky. At midday there isn't a cloud anywhere. (name1) is very gracious about winning. Then, just after lunch, a single small cloud drifts over and drops about nine raindrops directly on (name1)'s head. They argue happily all evening about who won. The wall gets a line and a dot.

Keep the Guessing Private:
(name1) just smiles and says nothing. The day turns out sunny, with a little wind after lunch. That evening (he/she) scratches a line on the wall, then, after a moment, a tiny extra mark next to it, which is (his/her) private way of saying "and (name2) was wrong." (He/she) has never been so pleased with a mark in (his/her) life.

68. The Emergency Snack
(picks an adult)

(name1) keeps an emergency snack hidden in the roof thatch: a little bundle of dried fruit, wrapped in a leaf, for a day when things are going badly. Today things are going badly. (name1) reaches up into the thatch, unwraps the leaf, and finds a very small lizard, extremely full, asleep in the middle of it.

Let the Lizard Keep It:
(name1) looks at the lizard. The lizard, slowly, opens one eye and looks back. "Well," says (name1), "at least one of us is having a good day." (He/she) puts the bundle back in the thatch, lizard and all. Somehow the bad day feels a little funnier now.

Tell (name2) About the Thief:
(name1) goes straight to (name2) to complain. "A lizard ate my emergency snack!" (name2) tries very hard not to laugh and fails completely. Then (he/she) shares (his/her) own lunch. "Next time," (he/she) says, "hide two snacks. One for you and one for the lizard."

69. The First Cold Swim
(picks an adult)

Every year, on the first cool morning of the season, (name1) walks into the sea before sunrise and swims out to the big rock and back. Nobody else does this. Nobody asked (him/her) to. (He/she) started years ago and has never missed one. This year, as (he/she) heads for the water, (name2) is waiting on the sand with (his/her) arms wrapped round (him/her)self. "Can I come?"

Say Yes:
They walk into the water together. (name2) makes a noise like a startled seabird at the first wave and keeps making it all the way to the rock. On the way back, though, the sun comes up over the water, and both of them stop swimming to watch. Afterward they sit wrapped in blankets, teeth chattering, grinning at each other.

Ask (him/her) to Watch This Time:
"Watch this year," says (name1) gently. "It's a bit of a quiet thing for me." (name2) nods and sits on the sand. (name1) swims to the rock and back alone, the way (he/she) always has. When (he/she) comes out, (name2) is holding a warm blanket and a hot drink. That part, (name1) decides, could become a tradition too.

70. Today's Word
(picks any villager aged 10 or older)

Every morning (name1) picks a word and tries to use it as often as possible all day. Yesterday's word was "sideways." Today's word is "magnificent." So far the fruit has been magnificent, the weather has been magnificent, and (name2)'s slightly lopsided basket has been called "a magnificent effort," which (name2) is still thinking about.

Ask About the Word Game:
(name2) asks what's going on. (name1) explains. (name2) is delighted and insists on choosing tomorrow's word. (He/she) picks "crumbly." The next day is very difficult for (name1), and (he/she) has never enjoyed a day more. [effect: (name1) celebrates]

Play Along Without Saying Anything:
(name2) starts using "magnificent" too. The fish is magnificent. The mud is magnificent. A passing crab is the most magnificent crab (he/she) has ever seen. (name1) narrows (his/her) eyes, says nothing, and the next morning chooses a word that is much, much harder to fit into conversation.

71. The Nose Scratch
(picks an adult)

Before (name1) makes any decision at all, (he/she) scratches (his/her) nose. Big decision, small decision, which fish to cook, which path to take: scratch, then decide. (He/she) has no idea (he/she) does it. Recently, though, a few villagers have noticed, and a rumor is going around that it's a secret signal.

Find Out About the Rumor:
(name2) asks, very quietly, what the signal means. (name1) has no idea what (he/she) is talking about. They work it out together, and (name1) laughs so hard (he/she) has to sit down. Then, without thinking, (he/she) scratches (his/her) nose to decide whether to get up. (name2) points at it in triumph.

Never Find Out:
(name1) goes about (his/her) day, scratching (his/her) nose before every choice. Each time, someone nearby looks around meaningfully. By evening a small group has decided the signal means "something interesting is about to happen." Oddly enough, several interesting things do. Nobody tells (name1).

72. The Good Spoon
(picks an adult)

(name1) has eaten every meal for years with the same carved wooden spoon. It has a little chip on the handle and fits (his/her) hand perfectly. Tonight it is gone. (name1) has looked everywhere. The bowl of stew is getting cold, and there are plenty of other spoons, and none of them are right.

Search One More Time:
(name1) turns the hut upside down. (name2) comes to help and, after a long search, finds the spoon in the water jar, where (name1) put it that morning after stirring something. (name1) holds it like a lost friend. The stew is cold by now. (name1) eats it anyway and says it's the best stew (he/she) has ever had.

Use a Different Spoon Tonight:
(name1) picks up an ordinary spoon and eats. It's fine. The stew is fine. Everything is fine. It's just a little bit wrong. The next morning the good spoon turns up in the water jar, and (name1) decides not to ask (him/her)self how it got there.

73. Thank You, Water
(picks an adult and a child aged 4-10)

Every time (name1) fills (his/her) jar at the water, (he/she) says "thank you" to it. Quietly, the way you'd thank someone passing you a bowl. Today (name2) is with (him/her) and wants to know: "Can the water hear you?"

Say You Don't Know:
"I don't know," says (name1), honestly. "Some people think so. Some people don't. I just like saying it." (name2) thinks about this, then leans over the water and says "thank you" too, very loudly, just in case it's a little hard of hearing.

Ask What (name2) Thinks:
"What do you think?" asks (name1). (name2) looks at the water for a long time. "I think it hears the fish," (he/she) says. "So it can probably hear us." (name1) agrees that this sounds reasonable. On the way home, (name2) thanks a puddle, two coconuts and a very surprised builder.

75. The Last Bite
(picks two adults)

(name1) always leaves the last piece of fruit in the bowl, "for whoever comes next." (He/she) has done it for years. Nobody ever eats it. The last piece sits there until it goes soft and gets thrown out, and then (name1) leaves a new last piece. Today (name2) walks in, sees it, and reaches for it.

(name2) eats the last piece in two bites. (name1) watches with an expression nobody has ever seen on (his/her) face before. "Was that alright?" asks (name2). "That," says (name1), "is the first time in years anyone has eaten it." "Well, it was very good." (name1) is absurdly pleased about this all day.

Explain the Habit:
(name1) explains about whoever comes next. (name2) stops, fruit halfway to (his/her) mouth. "But I'm whoever comes next," (he/she) says. (name1) has never thought of it that way. After a moment (he/she) nods solemnly, and (name2) eats the fruit with great ceremony.

76. The Same Story Every Night
(picks an adult and a child aged 3-8)

Every night (name1) tells (name2) the same bedtime story, about a little turtle who was looking for the perfect rock. (name2) knows every word. Tonight (name2) sits up and says, "Can I have a NEW story?"

Make Up a New One:
(name1) starts a story about a bird who wanted to learn to swim. It goes well for a while. Then, somehow, the bird meets a little turtle, and the turtle is looking for the perfect rock, and before (name1) knows it (he/she) is telling the old story again. (name2) is asleep before (he/she) notices. (name1) decides that counts as a new story.

Ask (name2) to Tell One Instead:
(name2) tells a long story about a crab who became the leader of the whole ocean and then had a nap. It has no ending, because (name2) falls asleep in the middle of it. (name1) sits in the dark for a while, wondering how the crab's nap went.

77. Waving at the Canoes
(picks an adult)

Whenever (name1) sees a canoe out on the water, (he/she) waves at it. Even when it's far too far away for anyone in it to see. Even when it might be a log. (name2) watches (him/her) waving enthusiastically at a dark speck on the horizon and asks why.

Explain:
"In case they're having a hard day," (name1) says. "Somebody should wave." (name2) looks at the speck. Then (he/she) waves too. That evening the village fishers come home, and one of them says, quite seriously, "Was that you two waving? We saw you from all the way out there. Made the whole afternoon." [effect: small food gain]

Admit You Don't Really Know:
"I've always done it," (name1) admits. "I don't know why." (name2) shrugs and waves at the speck too, just to see what it's like. The speck turns out to be a log. They both agree it seemed to appreciate it.

78. The Three Sneezes
(picks an adult; a single popup, no choice)

(name1) always sneezes exactly three times. Never one. Never two. Always three, each one louder than the last. The whole village knows it, and when (name1) sneezes at dinner, everyone counts along: "One! Two!" Then nothing. (name1) sits frozen, face scrunched up, waiting. Everyone waits with (him/her). The fire crackles. A baby somewhere starts to giggle. After a very long moment, (name1) relaxes, opens (his/her) eyes and says, "I think that's it." The whole fire bursts into applause. Then, while everyone is still clapping, the third sneeze arrives, the loudest one yet. Somebody drops a fish.

79. The Tidy Stack
(picks two adults)

Every evening (name1) stacks the village baskets by size, biggest on the bottom, smallest on top, all facing the same way. Tonight (he/she) arrives to find (name2) has already done it, "to help." The baskets are sorted by color.

Restack Them:
(name1) thanks (name2) warmly, waits for (him/her) to leave, and then restacks every basket by size. The next evening (he/she) finds them sorted by color again. This goes on for several days. Neither of them ever mentions it. It becomes, without anyone deciding so, a quiet sort of game.

Leave Them Sorted by Color:
(name1) looks at the stack for a long time. It's wrong. It's also, now (he/she) looks at it properly, rather pretty, like a little sunset made of baskets. (He/she) leaves it. It bothers (him/her) for exactly two days, and then (he/she) stops noticing.

80. The Lucky Side of the Bed
(picks an adult)

(name1) always gets up on the left side of the sleeping mat. Always. Today, still half asleep, (he/she) rolls off on the right side. (He/she) stands in the middle of the hut, staring at the mat, feeling that something important has gone wrong with the universe.

Lie Back Down and Get Up Properly:
(name1) lies back down, waits a moment, and rolls out on the left side. Much better. (name2), passing the doorway, sees the whole thing and decides it's none of (his/her) business. The day goes perfectly well, and (name1) is quite certain that's no coincidence.

Carry on with the Day:
(name1) goes about (his/her) work braced for disaster. Nothing happens. The fishing is ordinary. Lunch is ordinary. By evening (name1) is almost disappointed. "Wrong side of the mat," (he/she) tells (name2), "and nothing happened at all." "Maybe it's lucky too," says (name2). (name1) doesn't think so. (He/she) uses the left side tomorrow, just to be safe.
